The basics

What Is a Bank QR Code Maker?

A bank QR code maker turns your bank account details into a scannable QR image, so the person paying you never has to type your account number. You fill in who the account belongs to, which bank holds it and what the number is, and the tool encodes that into a square image you can send, print or pin anywhere.

In Nepal, the familiar way to get paid by someone is still the clumsy one: you type your account number into a chat, the sender copies it across into a banking app, and both of you hope the digits survived the trip. A bank QR code maker removes the typing step. The details travel as a picture instead of as text, and the sender scans it.

What the QR carries

Three things are always inside: the account holder name, a code identifying the bank that holds the account, and the account number. Two more are optional: a fixed amount in NPR, and a short remark such as a month, an invoice number or a room number. Leave the amount out and the sender types whatever they are paying. Put it in and the figure travels with the code.

Why typed account numbers go wrong

Account numbers are long strings of digits with no meaning to the eye, which is exactly the kind of thing people mistype. A digit gets dropped, two get swapped, a leading zero disappears when the number passes through a spreadsheet, or the number gets broken across two lines in a chat. Because nothing about a wrong number looks wrong, you tend to find out only afterwards, when someone asks whether the payment reached you. A scanned QR skips the retyping entirely, which is where the error was going to happen.

What it does not do

A bank QR code maker does not move money and does not touch your account. It only writes your details into an image. The transfer itself happens later, in the sender’s own banking or wallet app. The code carries the details you typed and nothing more: no password, no PIN, no login of yours. Sharing it is sharing your account details, which is what you would put on an invoice anyway, so share it with the people you actually want paying you.

That makes it useful for anyone who gets paid by more than one person: freelancers sending invoices, tuition and coaching classes collecting fees, a small shop that wants a code by the counter, landlords collecting rent, and anyone tired of retyping the same account number every time a friend settles up. The details are fixed once, checked once, and reused as many times as you want.

Compatibility

Where the QR Works

The QR is read by mobile banking apps and wallet apps that support scanning a recipient QR for a bank transfer. Whether a particular app accepts this particular QR is decided by that app and not by this tool, so test it with one scan before you print it or send it around.

Payments in Nepal do not run through a single app, so it helps to know the names you will meet. Support and wording differ from one app to the next, and the sender’s own screen is the only reliable test.

Bank mobile apps

Banks in Nepal run their own mobile apps, and they do not all use the same words for the same action. One app may say scan, another may say upload. Expect the wording on the sender’s screen to differ from what someone on a different bank’s app sees.

Wallet apps

eSewa, Khalti and IMEPay are wallet apps used in Nepal. Whether a wallet can scan a recipient QR and send to a bank account, and where that option sits in its menu, is set by the wallet itself. A QR generator has no say in it.

Shared QR rails

FonePay, ConnectIPS and NEPALPAY QR, which is operated by Nepal Clearing House Limited, are payment systems you will see named in Nepal. Which of them any given app connects to is set by that app and its bank.

A personal account QR is not a merchant QR

This tool makes a personal account QR. It carries your own account details so another person can send money into your account from their app, which is what you want for rent, fees, salary, refunds, split bills and family transfers. A merchant QR is a different thing. A registered FonePay or NEPALPAY merchant QR belongs to a business and is arranged through that business’s bank rather than through a free generator. If you run a shop and you need a merchant QR, ask your bank for it. This tool cannot issue one, and a QR made here is not a stand-in for one.

Safety

Is It Safe to Share a Bank QR Code?

Yes, and it is safe for a simple reason: a bank account QR carries the same details you already hand over when you ask someone to transfer money to you. Your account holder name, a code identifying your bank, your account number, and an amount or remark if you chose to add one. That is the whole of it. Point a QR scanner app at the code you just generated and you will read those fields straight back. It carries no PIN, no password, no mobile banking login, no card number and no one-time code, so there is nothing in it that could be used to take money out of your account. A QR is a set of details written as a picture, not a key. It tells a sender where to send money. It cannot fetch any.

So the useful question is not whether the code is dangerous, but who ends up holding the picture. Treat it the way you already treat your account number and you have covered most of it. Here is what actually goes wrong in practice.

Troubleshooting

If Your QR Does Not Scan

Start by scanning your own code, because that one test splits the problem in half. Point a QR scanner app at it. If your name, bank code and account number read back cleanly, the image is fine and the fault is in the data you entered or in how the sender is scanning. If nothing reads back at all, it is the image itself, and that is almost always compression, cropping or a poor screen shot rather than the code. Work down this list in order.

  1. Retype the account number with no spaces and no dashes. Copy it from your banking app rather than from memory, and keep every leading zero. This generator only checks that the box has a few characters in it, so a number carrying a stray space, a hyphen or a dropped zero still produces a scannable code. It just points at the wrong account. This is the most common cause, and it is the one that fails quietly, after a scan that looked fine.
  2. Use the account holder name the way your bank holds it. The name inside the code is what the sender reads on screen before they confirm. A nickname, initials or a spelling different from the one on the account makes them stop and message you to check, which is exactly the friction the code was meant to remove. Match the account, not your contact name.
  3. Send the original PNG, not a screenshot of it. Chat apps recompress photos and sometimes crop them, and a squeezed or blurred code loses the fine detail a scanner needs. Attach the downloaded file rather than pasting a screenshot, use the send-as-file or document option where the app offers one, and keep the white margin around the code intact when you crop or print. A code with its border cut off is much harder to read. If the sender has to scan your screen off another phone, turn the brightness up and angle it away from the glare.
  4. Make sure the sender is in the fund transfer flow. Some banking apps and wallets have more than one scanner. A scanner meant for paying at a shop counter is looking for a merchant code, so it may not read a recipient bank QR as a transfer at all. The sender wants the scanner that sits inside the transfer-to-another-bank part of their app rather than the one on the pay-a-shop screen.
  5. Check that your bank was actually selectable. In the bank list, the Class B development banks marked eSewa code needed are greyed out and cannot be chosen, because the code that identifies them is not in this tool yet. If that is your bank, no QR was generated and there is nothing to debug. Send your details as text for now.
  6. If the amount is wrong, remember it is baked in. Anything you put in the Amount and Remarks boxes is written into the image at the moment you generate, so a code made for one figure keeps carrying that figure every time it is scanned. For a code you plan to reuse or print, leave both boxes empty and let each sender type their own amount.
What this tool cannot do for you: it is an independent free generator with no connection to any bank, wallet or payment network, so it cannot confirm that an account exists, is open, or belongs to the name printed next to it. Only your bank can tell you that. With a payer who has never sent to you before, the safest sequence is still the old one: have them send a small amount first, confirm it landed in your account, then share the same code for the full payment.
